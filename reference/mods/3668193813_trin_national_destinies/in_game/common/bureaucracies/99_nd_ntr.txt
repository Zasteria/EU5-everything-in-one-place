# National Destinies: Maori (Aotearoa) bureaucracies
# Five historically-grounded institutions of pre-modern Maori governance, drawn
# from iwi-hapu-whanau society of the 14th-19th centuries through to the
# Kingitanga unification of 1858. The auto modifier grants 3 slots (half of
# the five, rounded up; vanilla gives this country none on the day it forms
# and a capital gives none unless it is a megalopolis), so three of the five
# can run at once. Each bureaucracy reflects a distinct face of
# Maori political life: the rangatira chiefly council, the tohunga learning
# class, the pa fortification system, the muru-utu customary law, and the
# Kingitanga unifying movement. The set is deliberately tribal-confederal in
# character (no centralising "imperial bureaucracy").
# Estates: every Maori pop of the 1337 setup is a tribesman, so the country
# has the tribes estate and the crown and no other on the day it forms.
# Each institution is aligned with one of those two on both sides where it
# has two sides. A second estate is named only where the text is about a
# class that can appear later (chiefs as nobles, tohunga as clergy, traders
# as burghers, cultivators as peasants); it counts for nothing until then.

# 1. Rangatiratanga: the rangatira (chief) and ariki (paramount chief) led
#    the iwi (tribe) and hapu (sub-tribe) by inherited mana and personal
#    leadership, advised by the koroua (elder council). Authority was not
#    coercive but consensual: a rangatira who lost mana lost followers. The
#    confederation of iwi under the formed state is held together by mutual
#    recognition of these chiefly councils, not by a single sovereign decree.

		global_separatism = 0.05
	}
}

# 2. Tohunga and Whare Wananga: the tohunga were specialists who held the
#    most tapu (sacred-restricted) knowledge of the iwi: whakapapa genealogy,
#    star and sea navigation, pounamu and timber carving, healing arts, and
#    the karakia ritual that opened or closed every undertaking. Their
#    apprentices learned in the whare wananga, the sacred learning houses
#    where knowledge was transmitted orally across generations. To dismantle
#    the tohunga class was to sever the lineage of memory itself.

# 3. Pa Network: the spine of Maori warfare. A pa was a hilltop or peninsula
#    settlement defended by ditches, palisades, fighting platforms, and (after
#    European contact) musket-loopholed terraces. Every iwi maintained
#    multiple pa keyed to seasonal campaigns and refuges, built and repaired
#    by communal labour drawn from the surrounding hapu. The pa-network gave
#    Aotearoa a defensive depth that the British found nearly impossible to
#    reduce in the New Zealand Wars of the 1860s, but the labour obligation
#    fell on the cultivators, who paid in kumara and sweat.

# 4. Muru me Utu: the customary law of restitution and reciprocity. Muru
#    was the ritualised plundering raid, by which an offended hapu took
#    property from the offender's kin to restore the balance broken by a
#    breach of tapu or insult to mana. Utu was the broader principle of
#    reciprocity that demanded every gift, injury, or honour be answered in
#    kind. Together they constituted a community-enforced legal order that
#    bypassed any chief's command: mana was preserved, but feuds endured
#    across generations and the central state's writ was correspondingly
#    weaker than its parchment claimed.

# 5. Kingitanga: the King Movement, born in 1858 when the chiefs of the
#    central North Island elected Te Wherowhero Potatau the first Maori king
#    at Ngaruawahia. Modelled in part on the British monarchy that the chiefs
#    had observed since the Treaty of Waitangi (1840), the Kingitanga aimed
#    to halt the alienation of land by uniting the iwi under a single
#    sovereign capable of negotiating with the Crown on equal terms. In
#    bureaucracy form it represents the institutional moment when iwi
#    confederation hardens into national kingship, gained at the price of
