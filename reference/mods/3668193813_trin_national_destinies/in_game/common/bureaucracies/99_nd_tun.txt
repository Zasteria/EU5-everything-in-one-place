# National Destinies: Tunis (TUN) bureaucracies.
# Four institutions, one for each power a ruler of Tunis could lean on: the
# Almohad shaykhs, the palace household, the freedmen sent out as governors,
# and the merchant families who farmed the revenue. The auto modifier grants
# 1 slot (main_menu/common/auto_modifiers/99_nd_tun.txt).
#
# The Hafsids hold the tag from 1337, so three of the four are Hafsid offices
# a sitting sultan could found on day one, and the fourth (the farms) runs
# from the Hafsid coral lease of 1446 to the beys.
#
# What is deliberately NOT here, because the same country already has it:
#   Vanilla 1.4, every Muslim country (bureaucracies/muslim.txt): the Bayt
#     al-Mal, the Hisba, the Mazalim court, the Diwan al-Insha, the Barid,
#     the Diwan al-Hajj, the Diwan al-Jund and the Shurta. Hence no treasury
#     (the Hafsid sahib al-ashghal), no market inspector, no amins of the
#     souks, no police of the capital (which is what the dey became under
#     the Husaynids).
#   Vanilla's Tunis content: the mahalla is the unit ability tun_tax_raid
#     ("Have our mahalla raid", unit_abilities_l_english.yml:60), the princely
#     governors are the reform tun_strong_familial_governors, and farming of
#     the LAND tax is the law policy efficient_tax_farming (laws/
#     00_religious.txt:16), which the Hafsids start with.
#   Carthage (99_nd_cth.txt), which a Tunisian country may form later: the
#     Andalusi secretaries, the qadi al-jama'a, the maritime customs bureau,
#     the ribat network, the corsair captains' guild.
#   The Almohads (99_nd_alm.txt): the talaba, the council of the shaykhs
#     (levy size, general skill, land morale, noble satisfaction), the office
#     of the 'alama, the mint. Block 1 below is the Hafsid office set over
#     those shaykhs and grants none of that council's keys.
#   This country's own advances and events (99_nd_tun.txt, nd_tun.txt): the
#     iqta of the Ku'ub, the souks, the Zaytuna, the deys and their ojak, the
#     Zwawa, the makhzen tribes.
#
# Vanilla's Tunis content reads centralization_vs_decentralization (the
# Hafsids start at 75, templates/hafsid_state.txt; the governors reform is
# locked above -50, the Ifriqiya Reunited decision asks -25 or lower), and
# aristocracy_vs_plutocracy starts at -50. Three blocks move one of the two.
#
# Values: vanilla's own scale on 1.4 (VANILLA_REFERENCE.md, "Advance Modifier
# Ranges", and a count of every vanilla site of each key on 2026-10-01).

# 1. Shaykh al-Muwahhidin, the Chief of the Almohads. Abu Zakariya (1229 to
#    1249) created the post: the ruler's deputy, to whom every Almohad shaykh
#    answered. Ibn Khaldun (Muqaddima, III.32) calls him the wazir of the
#    Hafsids, who "appointed and dismissed officials, led the army and
#    conducted its wars". Ibn al-Lihyani held it from 1295, ran the army and
#    foreign affairs, and took the throne himself in 1311. Ibn Tafragin, an
#    Almohad shaykh, set a boy of thirteen on the throne in 1350 and ruled in
#    his name for fourteen years.
#    Working: the Almohad companies muster (noble levies), the army keeps its
#    experience, and a succession the shaykhs have sworn to is harder to
#    contest. Starved: the kingmaker is his own candidate.
#    Allow: the shaykhs sat in the Kasbah of Tunis, the citadel the Almohads
#    laid out. Its mosque (Abu Zakariya, 1230 to 1233) was at first reserved
#    to those who lived inside it.
#    Estates: the Almohad shaykhs are the nobles. The Andalusi secretaries
#    who took the pen and the purse from them are the burghers.
tun_shaykh_al_muwahhidin_bureaucracy = {

# 2. The Hijaba, the office of the hajib. Ibn Khaldun (Muqaddima, III.32)
#    describes its rise under the Hafsids: first the steward of the ruler's
#    household, in charge of "salaries, allowances, garments, kitchen and
#    stable expenditures"; then, as the sultans withdrew from view, the one
#    door between the ruler and everyone else; at last holder of "the sword"
#    and of counsel as well. Ibn Tafragin took it in 1343. Ibn Khaldun held
#    it himself at Bejaia in 1365. Abu al-Abbas (1370 to 1394) abolished it
#    "and handled all his affairs himself".
#    Vanilla already calls a Tunisian head of cabinet the Hajib
#    (customizable_localization/00_customizable_localization.txt:444), so the
#    neutral effect makes that promotion cheaper, as the office of the
#    eristavi does for Georgia (laws/02_distribution_of_power.txt:405).
#    Working: the household is run to account and the court's business goes
#    through one hand. Starved: the door closes on the sultan, and the hajibs
#    of the governor princes follow the example of the one in the capital.
#    Estates: the office was the career of the secretarial families, many of
#    them Andalusi (burghers), and it was taken from the Almohad shaykhs.

# 3. The freedman qaids. Uthman (1435 to 1488) "refrained from giving high
#    office to fellow Hafsid dynasts and generally appointed royal retainers,
#    often renegade Europeans, as qaids or provincial governors". Bejaia went
#    from the sultan's cousins to such a qaid in 1452. Men outside the
#    dynasty were no safer for it: the minister Nabil ibn Abi Qattaya, who
#    had dominated the court early in the reign and whom the Italians called
#    "the first after the king", was put to death in 1453 and his wealth
#    confiscated. The source calls him a minister, not a provincial qaid.
#    Source for all three: the English Wikipedia article "Abu 'Amr 'Uthman",
#    which cites R. Brunschvig, "La Berbérie orientale sous les Hafsides",
#    1940, pp. 245 to 247 (read 2026-10-01; Brunschvig itself was not read).
#    That these men were freed slaves: "Christian captives of the pirates who
#    were not ransomed were sold as slaves. The men amongst them were
#    liberated when they became Muslim and found employment in the state,
#    especially in the army. In the fifteenth century some of them held
#    important offices, including the governorships of the provinces" (J.
#    Abun-Nasr, A History of the Maghrib in the Islamic Period, 1987, p. 131,
#    citing Brunschvig 1940, pp. 245 and 259 to 260; read 2026-10-02 in Google
#    Books snippets). Freedman and renegade are the same men.
#    The beys did the same with their mamluks: Yusuf Sahib al-Tabi, a
#    Moldavian mamluk bought at Constantinople in 1777, at thirteen, by Baccar
#    Djellouli of Sfax and given to the prince Hammuda in 1781, kept the seal
#    from 1782 and placed his own qaids across the country. He was struck
#    down before the Bardo on 15 September 1814 and died of it on 23 January
#    1815. (French and English Wikipedia, "Youssef Saheb Ettabaâ", the French
#    citing Ibn Abi Dhiaf; read 2026-10-02. The English says he was
#    "manumitted" in 1777, the French "racheté", bought: a boy given to a
#    prince four years later was not yet free, so this file says bought.)
#    This is the opposite of vanilla's reform tun_strong_familial_governors,
#    which costs the country a quarter of its land proximity
#    (land_cost_on_distance_from_capital_speed_propagation = -0.25). A man
#    who owns nothing the sultan did not give him carries the capital's writ
#    where a prince did not, so the funded effect is that same key, and the
#    drift is toward the centralization vanilla's Tunis decisions ask for.
#    Starved: the qaid keeps what he collects and the tribes find the forts
#    of the interior unpaid.
#    Allow: the household such men were raised in is vanilla's own advance
#    hafsid_ghulam (advances/region_maghreb.txt:225, age 1, the tag TUN).
#    Estates: the crown gains what the princes and shaykhs (nobles) and the
#    chiefs of the interior (tribes) lose.

# 4. The lizma, the farm of a revenue. What was farmed in Tunis was not the
#    land tax (vanilla's law has that) but everything else: the customs of a
#    port, the hides, the salt, the tobacco, the provisioning of the army,
#    and the caidates themselves. The Hafsids leased the coral of Tabarka to
#    a Barcelona merchant in 1446 and to Genoese from 1451 (sources in the
#    comment above nd_tun_coral_of_marsa_al_kharaz, advances/99_nd_tun.txt).
#    Under the beys the farmers were native merchant houses
#    who were governors and shipowners at once, the qaid-lazzams: the
#    Djellouli of Sfax from the 1600s, the Ben Ayed of Djerba from 1756.
#    Yusuf Sahib al-Tabi rebuilt the farms around his own clients from 1783
#    (French Wikipedia, "Youssef Saheb Ettabaâ": between 1783 and 1815 "il
#    modifie le fermage" and settles families of qaids who depend on him).
#    Working: the farmer pays before he collects, so more of the duties
#    charged on goods reaches the treasury (tariff_income is the share that
#    does; the base is 0.1, auto_modifiers/country.txt:30), and the state
#    can borrow against next year's farms. Starved: nobody checks the bids,
#    the farms pass from father to son, the country is squeezed, and the
#    farmers lend the bey his own money at interest.
#    Estates: the farmers are burghers. The peasants pay.
tun_lizma_bureaucracy = {
