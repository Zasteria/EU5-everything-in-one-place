# National Destinies: Lombard bureaucracies
# 6 institutions of the Duchy and State of Milan, from the communal
# Tribunale di Provvisione to the Theresian reform of the chamber courts.
# Deliberately none of them is a merchant tribunal or a commune executive:
# those belong to Florence (TUS) and Venice (ITA). What is Milanese here is
# the sovereign court that could refuse the prince's decrees, the chamber
# that sold fiefs and running water, the annona of a city of a hundred
# thousand, the oldest standing health board in Europe, and the archbishop's
# schools that taught the plain its letters.
#
# On the day of forming the country's own grant gives 3 places: 3 of 6
# institutions can run.
#
# All IDs are lmb_-prefixed to prevent collisions with other tags'
# bureaucracies (the engine loads 99_nd_*.txt alphabetically; last wins).

# 1. Senato di Milano. Louis XII erected it by the ordonnance of Vigevano
#    on 11 November 1499, seventeen members on the model of the French
#    parlements, and every later master of Milan kept it: Sforza, Spanish
#    and Austrian alike. It judged in last instance, its rulings binding
#    on the lower courts, and it registered sovereign decrees, refusing
#    those it held contrary to the law of the duchy. Milanese jurists
#    filled its benches and a seat was the summit of their careers.
#    Joseph II stripped its administrative powers in 1771 and abolished
#    it on 11 February 1786. Estates: the jurist patriciate and the
#    prelates who sat with it gain; the prince, whose will it can refuse,
#    loses. The entrenchment effect is the institution's own history:
#    287 years and an imperial edict to be rid of it.

# 3. Regalia delle Acque. Running water in the duchy belonged to the
#    prince. From the 1490s the Camera Ducale sold and leased it by the
#    ounce, measured at the bocche where a roggia left the Naviglio, and
#    a concession once bought could be resold or rented on. The revenue
#    was enormous: the Naviglio Grande alone was reckoned at 125,000
#    ducats a year in Leonardo's time, and courtiers, dignitaries and
#    charitable houses held the grants. In 1515 Massimiliano Sforza,
#    needing money against the French, sold the waters, beds and banks
#    of the Grande and the Martesana outright to the city of Milan.
#    The losers were the abbeys of Chiaravalle and Morimondo, whose
#    monks had cut the first canals and whose older claims the regalia

# 4. Tribunale di Provvisione. Milan's own magistracy: twelve citizens
#    whose powers the council fixed on 11 April 1280, presided from 1366
#    by a Vicar of Provision. It ran the annona (grain purchase, the
#    weight and price of bread, the markets, the streets and the city's
#    accounts). In 1515 Massimiliano Sforza let the city name 150
#    deputies to elect the vicar and the twelve; under Spain the governor
#    chose the vicar each year from six nobles proposed by the decurions.
#    It governed Milan until Joseph II abolished it in 1786. The contado
#    paid for the cheap loaf: requisition at fixed prices was the
#    countryside's standing grievance, and when the granaries failed the
#    city rioted anyway, as it did at San Martino in November 1628.

# 5. Ufficio di Sanita. Milan registered every death from 1452, each
#    corpse seen by a physician of the health office and reported by the
#    officiali delle bollette in the parishes; the series of registers
#    survives. The Lazzaretto rose outside Porta Orientale from 1488,
#    280 rooms around a court almost 400 metres on a side. Francesco II
#    Sforza raised the office to the Magistrato di Sanita on 13 May 1534,
#    and its health passes, sealed houses and cordons answered the
#    plagues of 1576 and 1630. Merchants hated the quarantine; the
#    archbishop's clergy defied its ban on assemblies to walk in
#    procession. Starved of funds it does not stop policing, it stops
#    knowing: 1630 ended in the hunt for the untori and the Column of

# 6. Scuole della Dottrina Cristiana. Castellino da Castello opened a
#    Sunday school at San Giacomo in Porta Nuova in 1536 and taught the
#    children of the poor their catechism together with reading, writing
#    and arithmetic. His Compagnia della Reformatione Christiana in
#    Charita of 1539 spread the model: fifteen schools when Ormaneto
#    arrived as vicar general in 1564, twice that within two years,
#    thirty at Castellino's death. Carlo Borromeo gave the congregation
#    San Dalmazio in 1577 and set the Oblates over it. This was the
#    Ambrosian church teaching Lombardy its letters, parish by parish,
#    and it answered to the archbishop rather than to the governor and
#    the Senate, who fought its jurisdiction for a generation.
